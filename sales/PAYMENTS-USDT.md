# Getting paid — and how USDT enters the conversation without killing the deal

For **Abdulrhman Thali, freelance web developer, Karm El-Zeitoun, Homs, Syria**. Written 2026-10-11 after the
first three emails went out. This is a working rule-sheet, not tax or compliance advice: a payment route you
actually own is worth one paid hour with an accountant who has done this before.

## The order of operations (this is the whole trick)

1. **Cold email:** never mention crypto. A village bakery reading "pay me in USDT" in an unsolicited first
   message stops reading — and "no VAT on my invoice, I'm based in Syria, I work remotely" is already enough
   information for message one. Crypto language in a cold email is the single fastest way to look like the
   spam you are not.
2. **They reply with interest.** Now you have a conversation, not a pitch.
3. **On the quote** (before the invoice), the payment line reads both ways:
   > **Payment:** 40 % to book the slot. Bank transfer in EUR (IBAN) — or, if that is easier for you,
   > **USDT (TRC-20 / BEP-20 / ERC-20)** at the EUR amount written on the invoice, sent to my address below.
   > Same number either way; no "crypto discount", no surcharge. Whichever you and your accountant prefer.
4. **Their consent in writing.** Not a handshake: the reply that says "we'll pay by USDT" is what makes it a
   documented settlement method inside a normal contract. If their accountant says no, take the bank transfer.
   You are not entitled to their convenience, and a client who half-agrees to crypto and then panics is worse
   than one who never heard the word.
5. **Invoice as normal** — sequential number, their name and address, the service, the amount in EUR,
   "no VAT charged (place of supply: your country)", and one extra line:
   *"Settled in USDT on [date], tx [hash], 1:1 against the EUR amount stated above."*

## The five practical rules

| Rule | Why |
|---|---|
| **Quote in EUR, settle in USDT — never the reverse** | If you invoice "3,857 USDT", a 4 % move either way is a fight. The obligation is a euro amount; the stablecoin is just the rail. |
| **Fix the rate on the invoice date, write the hash on the receipt** | Nobody can accuse you of FX games later, and you can prove the EUR value for your own books. |
| **TRC-20 / BEP-20 first, ERC-20 only for big tickets** | A €1,450 site does not deserve €18 of gas — and the client's surprise fee at the counter is the kind of detail that ends a first project badly. |
| **Check your own address is clean, and refuse anything that looks mixed** | Sanctions screening is on you and on whoever provides the wallet, not on the baker. If a client offers to pay from an "exchange account a friend owns", that's a no: it is their compliance problem and yours at once. |
| **Declare it as ordinary income** | USDT does not exempt anything; it is revenue at the EUR value on the day you receive it. If your local rules for crypto income are unclear, that's exactly the hour you pay the accountant for. |

## What to expect from the client's side (so you can answer quickly)

- **"Our accountant says no."** → "Understood — bank transfer in EUR is fine, send me the details your
  accountant is comfortable with and I'll invoice today." Have a real IBAN path ready, or you lose the job.
- **"Are you VAT registered?"** → "No — I'm an independent outside the EU, so my invoice has no VAT; you
  declare it under the reverse charge if you're VAT-registered. If you're under franchise en base / módulos,
  you'll owe local VAT on it that you can't recover — tell me and I'll price with that in mind."
- **"Where do the files live?"** → "Git repository, yours, and hosting in your name with your own login."
  (That answer is worth more to them than any payment method.)
- **"Can we pay half now, half in a month?"** → yes, but the deposit stays 40 % and go-live waits for the
  balance. Written in `sales/TERMS.md` clause 3, so you never argue it in the moment.

## What I will not put in a message

No "pay in crypto, it's easier and nobody asks questions". No fake EU entity, no borrowed IBAN, no
"invoice through my cousin's company". Not because it's risky for you — because it's the exact sentence that
turns a €1,450 website into a frozen account, and a bakery's bookkeeper is the last person on earth who should
be talked into a workaround. If the payment rails stay blocked, the honest move is to pause outreach and fix
the rails, not to hide them in the paperwork.
